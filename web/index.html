Constellation

Pulsar, Binary and Singularity: three playable pocket ribbon synths, modelled part by part from the public monotron, monotron DUO and monotron DELAY schematics and built on the Faust standard library. Nebula adds fourteen reverbs from Airwindows' open-source code.

Play either ribbon with a mouse or finger. The keys A to ] (A W S E D F T G Y H U J K O L P ; ' ]) play the instrument you touched last, marked "Keys"; the space bar toggles its Hold. "To Binary" and "To Singularity" patch an instrument into another's line in, so it plays through that one's filter (and Singularity's echo); the receiving instrument must be switched on. Chain Pulsar into Binary and Binary into Singularity to use all three, and any of them "To Nebula" for reverb. A MIDI keyboard plays the same instrument once connected. Drag knobs up or down. At full Peak a filter whistles on its own, without touching the ribbon.

Nebula: where the code comes from

Nebula runs fourteen reverbs by Chris Johnson of Airwindows, from his MIT-licensed source code. Each plugin's original C++ is compiled to WebAssembly unchanged, with a small stand-in for the VST plugin framework. A test builds the same C++ natively and confirms the browser version's output matches it, bit for bit in almost every case.

The selector's one-line descriptions are Chris Johnson's, from his Airwindopedia. Plugins that are fully wet by default start at 50% here, because an instrument patched in with "To Nebula" is heard only through Nebula.

To keep knob moves from clicking, each change crossfades over 20 ms from the running reverb to an exact copy of it with the new setting. Every reverb is made, and run once silently, before sound starts: making one while playing would stall the audio. A reverb you switch away from rings out silently in the background, so switching back to it doesn't bring back an old tail. With nothing playing into it and its tail gone, Nebula sleeps and costs nothing.

All the instruments and reverbs get their sines, exponentials and powers from musl's math library (MIT), compiled to WebAssembly along with them. Calling the browser's own Math.sin from WebAssembly creates a little garbage on every call; at several calls per sample, collecting it paused the audio often enough to crackle.
